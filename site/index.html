Reverse Vowels

Two rules for the same idea. Consonants, spaces and punctuation never move — only the vowels do. The second rule moves only clusters: two or more vowel letters in a row, the written shape of a spoken diphthong like ei, ou, eau. A vowel standing alone is a landmark and stays put.

Version 1

Every vowel is movable

Version 2

Only clusters of 2+ are movable

unchanged — fewer than two clusters to swap

What the algorithm sees

cluster — moves lone vowel — pinned
Why ICE comes back untouched

In ICE the I and the E are each alone on the page, separated by C. Version 2 pins both, so the word is returned exactly as given.

Spoken, it is a different story: ICE is /aɪs/, a real diphthong hidden inside one written letter. English spelling does not mark it, and ICE and SIT both show a lone I while sounding nothing alike. No letter-level rule can tell them apart. Catching the spoken vowel needs a pronunciation dictionary — CMUdict through nltk or pronouncing in Python — to map each word to phonemes first, reverse those, then spell the result back out, which is the lossy part.

Both rules are ported from the Python in reverse_vowels.py: reverseVowels and reverseSpokenVowels.
